\chapter{Introduction}
Imbuing intelligent agents with the capability for autonomous-decision-making in real-world environments is challenging. For decades, reinforcement learning has offered a robust mathematical framework for acquiring optimal behavioral skills through interactions within an environment to maximize cumulative rewards (Sutton and Barto 2014). The integration of deep neural networks as high-capacity function approximators has propelled RL even farther, leading to remarkable achievements across diverse domains, from mastering complex games to enabling basic robotic locomotion and manipulation (Chen et al. 2018). 

However, the widespread success of modern machine learning, as seen in areas such as computer vision and natural language processing, fundamentally hinges on the availability of large and highly diverse datasets. In the conventional RL paradigm, the acquisition of such extensive data becomes prohibitively impractical for real-world applications. For example, autonomous driving would need to practice in numerous cities for every model update, which is costly, time-consuming, let alone being unsafe. This inherent limitation created a gulf of generalization between lab-bound RL successes and the complex, open-world settings where intelligent decision-making is most critically needed.

Offline Reinforcement Learning emerges as a pivotal paradigm to bridge this gap. Offline RL focuses on enabling the training of policies solely from previously collected datasets, without additional online interaction. This approach holds immense potential for transforming vast amounts of historical data into robust, generalizable decision-making engines, much like how supervised learning revolutionized pattern recognition. The implications for fields such as robotics, healthcare, and autonomous driving are profound, as it offers a viable pathway to deploy sophisticated AI systems where active data collection is either unfeasible or too costly. Beyond these, offline RL can revolutionize domains such as operations research, logistics, power plant control, inventory management, and finance, all of which have rich historical data where active exploration is difficult or expensive (Levine et al. 2020).

Finally, taking a page out of the rise of vision language models, the ability to leverage large and diverse datasets in an offline setting further enables the development of foundational Vision-Language-Action models or generalist robot models. This means that pre-training on massive, heterogenous and potentially multi-modal datasets can lead to acquisition of broadly generalizable skills. These can then be efficiently fine-tuned for specific tasks in deployment. In a way, this paradigm allows bootstrapping on pre-trained visual language models to learn behaviors. Passive data can now be transformed into active intelligence by learned observations (Kim et al 2024).

\section{Background and Context}

Despite its immense promise, offline RL is plagued by algorithmic challenges. At its core, offline RL is fundamentally about making and answering counterfactual queries (Levine 2020). These are “what if” questions that involve forming hypotheses about what might happen if the agent were to carry out actions different from those observed in the training data. In other words, the ability to improve upon the behavior seen in the dataset, it must execute sequences of actions that diverge from the training data.  This inherent need for counterfactual inference strains convention machine learning tools, which are typically designed under the assumption that test-time queries come from the same distribution as the training data, independently and identically distributed.

Consequently, making such counterfactual queries results in distributional shift. When a learned policy attempts to take actions or visit states that deviate from those observed in the training data, conventional RL algorithms can produce erroneous and excessively optimistic Q-value estimate. This leads to policies that are brittle and perform poorly when deployed.

This issue is particularly exacerbated by the use of high-dimensional and expressive function approximators such as DNN. The errors can accumulate quadratically over long horizons and make stable learning difficult. Furthermore, real-world datasets, especially those derived from human demonstrations or varied sources, inherently exhibit multimodal action distributions. This means that there are often multiple valid ways to perform a given task or different sequences of actions to achieve a long-horizon goal. Traditional behavior cloning methods frequently struggle to capture this multimodality effectively, resulting in jerky, hesitating, or stuck motions and failing to generalize beyond narrowly observed demonstrations. This inadvertently average across modes and leads to indecisive behaviors.

In recent years, generative modeling, particularly with denoising diffusion probabilistic models, has shown remarkable capability in generating high-quality and diverse images. These models learn by iteratively refining random noise into coherent output and offer significant advantages in addressing distributional shift when applied in robotics, especially in behavior cloning:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Diffusion models gracefully capture high-dimensional and multimodal action spaces, as evidenced by their success in image and video generation.
	\item They exhibit impressive training stability, requiring little task-specific hyperparameter tuning compared to models like generative adversarial networks. 
	\item The most surprising property is temporal compositionality. The models can essentially “stitch together” in-distribution subsequences from training data to generalize to novel tasks.
\end{enumerate}

This enables counterfactual queries because planning can now be framed as a sampling conditioned on desired outcomes. Just like in image generation, the iterative sampling process of diffusion models allows for flexible conditioning. This allows a single diffusion model to be used for multiple tasks in the same environment, even for tasks unseen during training.
