\chapter{Introduction}
Imbuing intelligent agents with the capability for autonomous-decision-making in real-world environments is challenging. For decades, reinforcement learning has offered a robust mathematical framework for acquiring optimal behavioral skills through interactions within an environment to maximize cumulative rewards (Sutton and Barto 2014). The integration of deep neural networks as high-capacity function approximators has propelled RL even farther, leading to remarkable achievements across diverse domains, from mastering complex games to enabling basic robotic locomotion and manipulation (Chen et al. 2018). 

However, the widespread success of modern machine learning, as seen in areas such as computer vision and natural language processing, fundamentally hinges on the availability of large and highly diverse datasets. In the conventional RL paradigm, the acquisition of such extensive data becomes prohibitively impractical for real-world applications. For example, autonomous driving would need to practice in numerous cities for every model update, which is costly, time-consuming, let alone being unsafe. This inherent limitation created a gulf of generalization between lab-bound RL successes and the complex, open-world settings where intelligent decision-making is most critically needed.

Offline Reinforcement Learning emerges as a pivotal paradigm to bridge this gap. Offline RL focuses on enabling the training of policies solely from previously collected datasets, without additional online interaction. This approach holds immense potential for transforming vast amounts of historical data into robust, generalizable decision-making engines, much like how supervised learning revolutionized pattern recognition. The implications for fields such as robotics, healthcare, and autonomous driving are profound, as it offers a viable pathway to deploy sophisticated AI systems where active data collection is either unfeasible or too costly. Beyond these, offline RL can revolutionize domains such as operations research, logistics, power plant control, inventory management, and finance, all of which have rich historical data where active exploration is difficult or expensive (Levine et al. 2020).

Finally, taking a page out of the rise of vision language models, the ability to leverage large and diverse datasets in an offline setting further enables the development of foundational Vision-Language-Action models or generalist robot models. This means that pre-training on massive, heterogenous and potentially multi-modal datasets can lead to acquisition of broadly generalizable skills. These can then be efficiently fine-tuned for specific tasks in deployment. In a way, this paradigm allows bootstrapping on pre-trained visual language models to learn behaviors. Passive data can now be transformed into active intelligence by learned observations (Kim et al 2024).

\section{Background and Context}

Despite its immense promise, offline RL is plagued by algorithmic challenges. At its core, offline RL is fundamentally about making and answering counterfactual queries (Levine, 2020). These are “what if” questions about what might happen if the agent were to carry out actions different from those in the dataset. In other words, for an agent to improve upon the behavior policy, it must be able to execute action sequences that diverge from the training data. This inherent need for counterfactual inference strains conventional machine learning tools, which assume that test data is drawn from the same distribution as training data. Consequently, making such counterfactual queries results in distributional shift. When a learned policy deviates from the training data, conventional RL algorithms can produce erroneous and excessively optimistic Q-value estimates, leading to brittle policies that perform poorly when deployed.

This instability is particularly exacerbated by high-dimensional function approximators such as Deep Neural Networks (DNNs). The errors can accumulate over long horizons, making stable learning notoriously difficult (CITE HERE). Furthermore, real-world datasets, especially those from human demonstrations, inherently exhibit multimodal action distributions; there are often multiple valid ways to perform a task. Traditional methods like behavior cloning struggle to capture this multimodality, often averaging across modes and leading to indecisive or jerky behaviors that fail to generalize.

To address the challenge of multimodality, generative modeling, particularly with denoising diffusion models, has emerged as a powerful approach. By framing planning as a conditional sampling process, diffusion models can learn and represent complex, multi-modal distributions directly from data. This is a critical capability for robotics, where a task may have multiple valid solutions, such as different grasps for an object or various paths around an obstacle. By capturing the entire distribution of expert solutions, these models can enhance a robot's versatility and ability to generalize.

However, the very properties that make diffusion models powerful for generation introduce fundamental challenges for their deployment in real-time robotic systems. These models are built upon a stochastic, iterative framework that imposes a significant computational burden, leading to high-latency inference incompatible with dynamic control loops. More importantly, their stochastic nature is fundamentally misaligned with the deterministic physical laws and hard safety constraints that govern robotic motion, calling for an alternative generative paradigms.

Therefore, this dissertation presents Flow Planner, a hierarchical framework built upon flow matching, a powerful alternative that directly addresses the core limitations of diffusion models. Flow matching learns a deterministic transformation governed by an Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) rather than a Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE). This fundamental distinction makes it not merely an incremental improvement but a paradigm inherently aligned with the deterministic, low-latency requirements of physical robotic systems.

\section{Objectives}

The primary aim of this dissertation is to develop and validate a novel, deterministic framework for behavior cloning in offline reinforcement learning, leveraging the principles of flow matching. The specific objectives established to achieve this aim are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
	\item To design and validate a systematic methodology for curating offline reinforcement learning datasets from actively trained agents.
	\item To design and implement "Flow Planner," a modular framework that utilizes flow matching for the generation of trajectories from offline data.
	\item To analyze the impact of various conditioning mechanisms on the precision and generalizability of the trajectories generated by the framework.
	\item To evaluate the performance of the "Flow Planner" framework, benchmarking its core attributes such as inference speed and policy effectiveness against diffusion models.
\end{itemize}

\section{Achievements}
This dissertation's primary contribution is the development and validation of Flow Planner, a novel and modular framework for deterministic behavior cloning. The key achievements are:


\begin{itemize}
	\item A Data Curation Pipeline and Benchmark Datasets: A flexible pipeline was designed and implemented for curating offline reinforcement learning datasets. This was demonstrated by producing three distinct datasets from PPO agents in Box2D environments, with the system designed for easy xetension to other agents and environments.
	\item A Modular Framework for Behavior Cloning: The Flow Planner framework was developed to directly address the high latency and stochasticity that limit current diffusion-based BC approaches. Its modular architecture is a key feature, allowing for the flexible optimization of its different components.
	\item Analysis of the Flow Planner Framework: This work provides a comprehensive analysis of the architectural choices essential for applying flow-matching models to behavior cloning. The findings highlight the critical role of conditioning mechanisms and model architecture, offering practical guidance for future implementations.
	\item Successful Proof-of-Concept Validation: The Flow Planner framework was successfully validated in the LunarLander continuous control environment. This proof-of-concept demonstrated the ability to generate effective policies with significantly lower inference latency than baseline approaches.
\end{itemize}

\section{Overview of Dissertation}
This dissertation is structured as follows:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Chapter 2 provides the theoretical background for this work. It begins by reviewing foundational concepts in offline reinforcement learning and methods for addressing distributional shift. The chapter then provides an overview of Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models, unifying these concepts by framing them through the lens of continuous flows to introduce flow matching. It concludes by reviewing applications of diffusion for trajectory synthesis and related state-of-the-art attempts at integrating these models directly into reinforcement learning.
	\item Chapter 3 details the methodology for data curation and introduces the experimental environments. This includes a technical discussion on the implementation of the data pipeline using Gymnasium wrappers and the Minari library for dataset management.
	\item Chapter 4 presents the core technical contribution of this dissertation. It details the different approaches o model the control problem and introduces the proposed hierarchical planning framework, "Flow Planner," along with its implementation details using Meta’s flow matching library.
	\item Chapter 5 provides a comprehensive analysis and discussion of the empirical results. This includes an investigation of various conditioning mechanisms and a benchmark comparison of the framework's performance against baseline models.
	\item Chapter 6 concludes the dissertation by summarizing the key contributions, discussing the implications of the findings, and outlining promising directions for future research.
\end{itemize}
